02

Vanishing points

A vanishing point is a direction, not a place.

Put your eye at the origin. Take any line in the scene and walk it to infinity. Its image converges on the point where a ray from your eye, parallel to that line, pierces the picture plane. Note what dropped out: the line's position. Only direction survives.

So every line sharing a direction — every curb, every rail, every brick edge — shares one VP. The picture plane is a map of directions.

Point count is a count, not a system

1-, 2- and 3-point aren't three methods. They're how many of a box's three edge directions are not parallel to the picture plane. A direction parallel to the plane never pierces it — its VP is at infinity, so it stays parallel on paper.

  • 1-point — square-on. Width and height parallel to the plane; only depth converges.
  • 2-point — box rotated. Both horizontals converge, onto the horizon. Verticals still parallel.
  • 3-point — head tilted. The picture plane is no longer vertical, so verticals converge too.

1-point is a poor teacher: VP, centre of vision and horizon all pile onto one dot and you can't see which concept is doing the work.

03

The horizon

The horizon is a fact about you, not about the world. Nothing in the scene is the horizon.

It's the locus of horizontal directions. Proof, in two lines: take any horizontal direction and walk it forever — you stay at your eye height, because that's what horizontal means to a level eye. The parallel ray from your eye never leaves eye height, so it pierces the plane at eye height. Every horizontal direction, no exceptions, one line.

It has a height but no distance. Stand up, it rises. Sit, it drops. Walk toward it and nothing happens — it's at infinity. It's welded to your eye.

The eye-level cut

Which means the horizon tells the viewer where their own body is. If it cuts a doorway at the knee, your reader is a toddler, or lying on the floor. You aren't describing the door. You're positioning a skull.

Horizon ≠ centre of vision

Constantly collapsed, genuinely different: the horizon is a line — where you are. The centre of vision is a point — where you're aimed. They coincide only when you look level.

04

Inclined planes

Since the horizon is the set of horizontal directions, any VP off the horizon belongs to a direction that isn't horizontal. Ramps, stairs, roof planes. And the method falls out for free:

An incline's VP sits directly above the VP of the horizontal direction it follows. Same left-right position, raised by h = |SP→V| · tan θ. Descending, lower it by the same amount.

A staircase is the same object with the tread/riser detail added. Nosings go to V↑. Risers are vertical planes whose horizontal edges are, still, horizontal — so those go to the horizon.

05

Depth spacing

Angles are the easy half. How far apart the fence posts go is what people get wrong, and it needs no VP access at all — which matters, because at 8×10 you rarely have any.

Why it works

The line from post 1's bottom to post 3's top is the diagonal of a real-space rectangle. Its centre sits at post 2 — horizontally because post 2 is halfway, vertically at post 2's midpoint. Projection preserves incidence, so the construction survives into the image intact.

Pass criterion, pinless

Tile the floor in squares, then lay a straightedge along the diagonals of tile 1 → 2 → 3 going back. Those are one straight line in real space, so they must be collinear on paper. If the straightedge kisses all three, your depth rate is right. If the diagonal VP happens to be on the plate, pin it as a second opinion — every tile diagonal should hit it.

06

Circles & ellipses

The gap that perspective study never covers, and the one that decides whether a shell, a wheel or a cup reads as solid.

Why the centres differ

The near and far edges of the square are parallel to the picture plane, so their tangent points form a genuine diameter, and the ellipse's centre is its midpoint. But the side edges converge — so their tangent points are not a diameter. The circle's centre lands where the diagonals cross, further back. Every construction that assumes the ellipse's centre is the circle's centre inherits this error.

8-point construction

  1. Draw the square in perspective.
  2. Diagonals → true centre.
  3. Through it, the two perspective midlines → four tangent points on the sides.
  4. Four more points along the diagonals, at the perspective image of 0.707 of the half-diagonal.
  5. Draw through all eight. Do not draw a symmetric ellipse and hope.
Application — shells and spiralsA conch is a stack of ellipses growing along a curving axis, each one's minor axis aligned to the local axis direction. Establish the axis curve first, then hang the ellipses on it. The shell becomes constructible instead of hopeful.

08

Armature

The rule of thirds is a grid dropped onto any rectangle. The armature is generated by the rectangle's own proportion. Nothing is chosen — you run the construction and the rectangle tells you where its structural lines are.

Four families, strongest first

  1. Diagonals — corner to corner.
  2. Eyes — from each remaining corner, drop a perpendicular onto each diagonal. The foot is an eye. Four total.
  3. Reciprocals — extend those perpendiculars past the eye to a side. That cuts off a rectangle of the same proportion, rotated 90°.
  4. Rabatment — inscribe a square flush at each end. The far edge of each is a rabatment line.

Medians are technically armature and are the weakest thing in the system. Dead-centre bisection is inert.

The eye is a spiral pole

Cut the reciprocal, then run the construction inside it. Then inside that one. The nested reciprocals shrink and rotate and converge — on the eye. The eye is the pole of the logarithmic spiral the rectangle generates from its own proportion. Equivalently, and more usefully at the board: the eye is where the diagonal crosses the reciprocal's diagonal.

Why the rule of thirds "works"

Now you can see the trick. For 2:3, rabatment coincides with the thirds exactly, and the eyes come within 0.026 of the height. 2:3 is the most common photographic and drawing ratio in the world. The rule of thirds is a degenerate approximation that happens to be nearly right for the one ratio everybody uses — and then gets generalised to ratios where it's simply wrong. For φ, the eyes sit at 0.276 and 0.724. Thirds miss badly.

Application — plate seamsThree 8×16 plates on a 16×24 put the seams at 8″ and 16″ — dead on the rabatment lines, because for 2:3 rabatment is the thirds. Two 8×24 plates put a single seam on the vertical median: the weakest line in the system, carrying the most physically conspicuous feature on the piece. The eyes sit 0.62″ inside each seam, so you can play them against each other — a focal event on the eye, the seam running just past it. Two structural lines slightly out of phase generate tension neither has alone.

Media characterization

Typical values not suppliedEverything above is geometry and holds for any hand. This section does not. It has to be measured on your paper, with your pens, by you — and then it's worth more than anything else in this document.

Why a printed grey scale is a lie

In pen work the mark and the value are not separable. A generic 9-step ramp tells you nothing about where your hatching stops getting darker and starts getting muddy. That threshold is the single most useful number in your kit, and it's different for every nib and paper.

Table 2 — Characterization procedure
TestMethodExtract
Ink rampsEach mark type — hatch, cross-hatch, contour hatch, stipple, scribble — pushed from lightest to your true black in one strip.Where each type tops out, and where it goes muddy rather than dark. These differ.
Black pointDarkest achievable per tool, per paper.Your actual dynamic range. Compose inside it.
Pencil pressureOne colour, light → full pressure, in a strip.Usable pressure band before the tooth loads up.
Pencil layersSame colour, 1 → 6 layers at constant light pressure.Layers vs. pressure give different darks. Know which.
Palette valuesEvery pencil, full and light, labelled. Then photograph and desaturate.Which colours are secretly the same value. This is why passages go flat no matter how much chroma you throw at them.
Frottage responseColour over a rubbed texture at several pressures.Which pressures ride the texture and which kill it. Exists in no book.

Notan before any of it: 3 or 4 values, thumbnail sized. If the composition doesn't hold at four values it will not be rescued at forty.

The 8×10 adjustment, generalised

VPs don't scale with the page. The cone ties d to your subject, so d ≥ 1.72R and the diagonal VP sits at d — always at least 1.72× the subject radius away, no matter how small the paper gets. In a bound book you can't pin off the edge. So:

At sketchbook scale you stop pinning and start setting angles. Slope to the off-plate VP → arctangent → the triangle. This is where that tool pays rent.

Corollary worth knowing: a construction VP only lands on the plate if the subject radius is under about 58% of the half-width. Push the CV off-centre and you buy room on one side.

11

Caveats & open items

  • Armature is contested history. Hambidge's claim that Greek vases were designed on dynamic symmetry is disputed. Bouleau's readings of the Old Masters are post-hoc — find an armature line near anything if you're willing to look. Treat this as a generator of non-arbitrary choices, not a law of beauty. Its value is that when you place something on an eye, the rectangle can justify the decision. That's all, and it's enough.
  • The cone of vision is a guideline you will break on purpose. Dramatic worm's-eye is a cone violation. Breaking it knowingly is a different act from breaking it by accident, and only the second one shows.
  • θ in §4 is the real incline, not the angle you measure at the near corner of the drawing. Easy to conflate, wrong every time.
  • §9 is blank on purpose and will stay wrong until you make the swatches.
  • Horizon height and VP spread are independent levers. Height says where the viewer's body is; spread says how close they're standing. You can be a child far away or a giant up close. Decide both.

Leave the last pages of the book blank. The reference entries worth keeping are the ones written after getting burned by something.
